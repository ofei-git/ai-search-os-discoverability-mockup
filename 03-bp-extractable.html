Lesson 2.6 Best Practice

Make your priority content extractable from raw HTML

Written by Michael Ofei Reviewed by Kevin Indig Updated May 20, 2026 Read time ~5 minutes

Many modern sites — especially those built with React, Vue, Next.js, or similar JavaScript frameworks — render their content in the browser rather than serving it as static HTML. Most AI crawlers don't execute JavaScript, so they see an empty shell instead of your content. Making your priority content extractable from raw HTML means confirming the words, products, and answers AI systems need are present in the page source before any JavaScript runs.

Why it works

The two crawler paths from earlier in the course — training-time and retrieval-time — both depend on what's in the raw HTML response, not what gets assembled in a user's browser. When a page's content only renders after JavaScript runs, AI crawlers tend to miss it. The citation never happens, because the content was never visible to begin with.

This affects more sites than most teams expect: marketing pages built on Next.js without proper server-side rendering, product pages that lazy-load via fetch after the initial paint, content gated behind interactive components. From a human visitor's perspective the site looks fine. From a crawler's perspective there's nothing to read.

What an AI crawler sees · client-rendered
Empty shell — no content to cite
<body>
  <div id="app"></div>
  <script src="app.js"></script>
</body>
// content loads after the crawler leaves
What an AI crawler sees · server-rendered
Real content — readable, citable
<body>
  <h1>How AI search works</h1>
  <p>AI systems retrieve...</p>
  <article>...full body...</article>
</body>

Vercel's research on AI crawler behavior tracked over 500 million crawl requests from GPTBot, ClaudeBot, and similar agents — none executed JavaScript on the pages they fetched.1 Independent testing from Onely and Search Engine Land has confirmed the same pattern across major AI bots.2 Brands serving content server-rendered or via static HTML appear consistently in cross-platform citation studies; JS-dependent SPAs (single-page applications) appear less often.

How to apply

  • Open your priority pages with JavaScript disabled in your browser. What's still visible is roughly what most AI crawlers see.
  • For client-rendered routes, add server-side rendering (SSR), static site generation (SSG), or pre-rendering for AI user agents.
  • Confirm the rendered HTML includes the actual content, not placeholder shells, skeleton loaders, or loading states.
  • Re-test after every major framework upgrade or template change. SSR can silently regress.

Watch out for

  • Some AI crawlers are starting to render JavaScript (Google's AI Overviews via Googlebot, for example), and full rendering may become standard within 12-18 months. For now, treat server-side rendering as the baseline — the crawlers that don't render JS won't be a niche group for at least the next year.
  • Prioritize the pages that matter most for citation: brand pages, product pages, definitive how-to guides, original research. Don't try to refactor everything at once.
