Lesson 2.8 Best Practice

Set a deliberate AI crawler policy, bot by bot

Updated May 19, 2026 Read time ~5 minutes Layer Discoverability

AI crawler policy isn't a default — it's a decision. Some sites block AI bots without realizing it, through inherited CDN rules, overzealous WAF settings, or older robots.txt entries written before AI bots existed. Others allow everything by default, without thinking through the tradeoffs. Setting a deliberate AI crawler policy means auditing what your current policy actually is, then choosing — bot by bot — whether each major AI crawler (Google, ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude) should be allowed, blocked, or limited.

Why it works

Most AI search systems get content through two main paths: training-time crawls (the bulk sweeps that build the model's underlying knowledge) and retrieval at query time (the live fetches an AI assistant makes when answering a user's specific question). Both depend on their crawlers being able to reach your pages. Block the crawler and your content drops out of those paths, even if it's the best answer on the web.

The two paths AI systems use to reach your content. Block either crawler and you drop out of that path.

Deliberation matters because the right answer isn't universal. A medical publisher might block training-time crawlers (concerned about content being reproduced without context) while keeping retrieval-time fetches open (so users can find specific articles). A content marketing site might allow everything (to maximize citation). Both are valid stances. The failure mode is making no decision — defaulting to whatever robots.txt was written before AI crawlers existed.

The crawler documentation is public: OpenAI's GPTBot, Anthropic's ClaudeBot, Perplexity's PerplexityBot, and Common Crawl's CCBot all respect robots.txt. Independent tracking from Originality.ai's bot block report shows rising publisher blocks against these crawlers; brands that maintain deliberate access policies continue to appear in cross-platform citation studies.

How to apply

  • Audit your current robots.txt for AI-specific user agents (GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot, Google-Extended, CCBot). Note what's allowed, blocked, or unmentioned.
  • Decide per-bot whether each should be allowed, blocked, or limited to specific directories. Document the reasoning.
  • Confirm your CDN and WAF rules align with the decision — a common silent failure that contradicts the robots.txt intent.
  • Schedule a recurring re-check. New bots appear, naming conventions shift.

Watch out for

  • Some AI bots fetch for training, others for live retrieval. You can allow one and not the other. The deliberation is per-bot and per-purpose.
  • Access is one part of the picture. Even with the right policy in place, content still has to be discoverable, well-structured, and worth retrieving before AI systems cite it.
