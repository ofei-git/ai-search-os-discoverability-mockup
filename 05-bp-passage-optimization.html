Lesson 2.7 Best Practice

Passage-level content optimization

Written by Michael Ofei Reviewed by Kevin Indig Updated May 20, 2026 Read time ~6 minutes

Most AI assistants don't retrieve your full page when they answer a user's question — they retrieve passages. A few hundred tokens lifted from somewhere in your content, ranked against the query, fed to the model. Passage-level optimization means structuring your content so semantically coherent chunks survive that retrieval process. Self-contained passages, entity-dense paragraphs, clear topic sentences — they're what makes individual sections of your content quotable on their own.

Why it works

Traditional SEO optimizes for whole-page ranking. Meta description, internal links, page authority — all about how the page performs as a unit.

AI retrieval is different. The model doesn't care about your page as a whole. It cares about which specific 200-500 tokens best answer the query. That's the RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) pipeline at work: content gets chunked, embedded, ranked, and the top-ranking chunks get fed into the model's context window.

A strong page with weak internal structure can get partially cited or, worse, cited without the framing that gives it meaning. If a paragraph references "the company" without naming it, and that paragraph gets retrieved as a standalone chunk, the user sees a citation that's about… someone. The chunk failed.

Pronoun-heavy, entity-poor
Loses meaning when retrieved

"The market has been changing rapidly. We've seen significant growth this year. As a result, the team has decided to invest more heavily in this space."

Entity-dense, self-contained
Stands on its own when retrieved

"AI search traffic to Backlinko grew 340% from January to April 2026. In response, the Backlinko editorial team is prioritizing passage-level content optimization across the top 200 ranked pages."

Cloudflare's research on serving content as Markdown for AI agents found roughly an 80% token reduction with no loss of usable information1 — a direct argument for clean, well-structured passages.

Liu et al.'s "Lost in the Middle" paper showed LLMs weight information at the start and end of retrieved contexts more heavily than the middle2, a pattern later corroborated by MIT researchers.3 Kevin Indig's analysis of 1.2 million ChatGPT responses found that 44.2% of citations came from the first third of source pages4 — the part most likely to be retrieved as a self-contained chunk.

How to apply

  • Lead each section with a clear topic sentence that names what the section is about. The opening line should be retrievable on its own.
  • Keep paragraphs short — 60-100 words — so passages stand alone.
  • Use semantic HTML: proper heading hierarchy (H2, H3), bullet lists where appropriate, blockquotes for quoted material. Structural markers help AI systems chunk cleanly.
  • Front-load your most important content. The first third of your page is doing the heaviest citation work.
  • Name entities explicitly in each passage. Pronoun-heavy writing loses meaning when a chunk is lifted out of context.

Watch out for

  • This isn't about writing for machines. Readers still come first — if your content reads like a list of disconnected paragraphs, you've over-corrected. The goal is content that works as continuous prose and survives chunking. Most well-written non-fiction already does this.
  • Don't try to refactor every page. Spend the editorial effort on the pages that matter most for citation — most-trafficked, highest-authority, key brand pages. Refresh-and-restructure is real work; treat it that way.
